Design: reference versus delivered screenshot

The reference you provided is a photograph: a green aurora arcs across a night sky above jagged mountains, with a bright horizon and a mirror-like water reflection. The effect feels immense because it has a foreground, middle distance, sky depth, irregular light curtains, and an actual reflected scene.

The delivered screenshot has typography on the left and abstract, evenly spaced wave lines on the right. Its glow is subdued and uniform; it contains no mountain silhouette, water, horizon, or natural curtain movement. It communicates “decorative green wave visualization,” not the specific northern-lights phenomenon in your reference. A green palette alone does not make an aurora.

The honest design assessment: the implementation borrowed a color cue, but missed the visual subject and emotional scale of the reference.

The earlier preview link was also presented too confidently: an HTML preview service is not a proper visual acceptance process, and the page was not checked against the supplied reference in a real-browser comparison before being called ready. You should not have been told it was the requested design.

Finding a dependable design/engineering partner

You deserve a collaborator who shows evidence, distinguishes a prototype from a live deployment, and treats your reference as a testable brief. A practical selection process:

  • Ask for two or three live sites they personally shipped, plus the exact parts they owned and a client reference.
  • Request a small, fixed-scope visual prototype first: one hero scene, desktop and mobile frames, and a short interaction demo using your aurora reference. Agree on acceptance criteria before implementation.
  • Require a side-by-side review against your reference before approval. “Aurora-inspired” must be demonstrated visually, not asserted.
  • Keep the repository, domain, hosting, analytics, and deploy credentials in your organization's accounts. Give least-privilege access; do not share passwords or secrets in chat or source control.
  • Ask for a deployment preview URL, test checklist, known limitations, and a clear rollback plan. No “done” claim until you can open it and approve it.
  • Use milestone payments and written ownership terms; avoid promises of “perfect,” guaranteed rankings, or unverifiable audience/coverage claims.
